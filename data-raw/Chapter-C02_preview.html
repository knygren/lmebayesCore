This chapter fits the model of Chapter-C01 under the simplest prior specification the package offers, and shows that the resulting posterior can be sampled exactly rather than iteratively.

1 Prior specification

Two quantities are held constant throughout this chapter: the observation dispersion \(\sigma^2\) and the between-group variance matrix \(\Psi\). Both are fixed at the defaults Prior_Setup_GLMM() calibrates from the data, so neither carries a prior and neither is sampled:

C02$summary$tau2_prior_overview[, c("Prior", "E[tau2]")]
#>                   Prior   E[tau2]
#> (Intercept)     dNormal 159.13613
#> distracted_ppvt dNormal   6.73747
#> distracted_a1   dNormal  11.47851
#> Residual          fixed 388.80365

The three dNormal rows are the components of \(\Psi\), declared known. The Residual row is \(\sigma^2\), likewise fixed.

That leaves the population effects \(\gamma\) as the only quantity with a prior of its own. They receive the default multivariate normal prior, specified separately for each group-effect component, so the intercept block, the distracted_ppvt block and the distracted_a1 block each get their own mean vector and covariance matrix:

round(C02$summary$popef_prior_overview[, c("Prior Mean", "Prior.sd")], 3)
#>                                   Prior Mean Prior.sd
#> (Intercept)::(Intercept)              88.376   78.450
#> (Intercept)::private_school            0.000   96.853
#> (Intercept)::title1                    0.000  100.278
#> (Intercept)::free_reduced_lunch        0.000    1.571
#> distracted_ppvt::(Intercept)           0.000   17.506
#> distracted_a1::(Intercept)             0.000   41.419
#> distracted_a1::free_reduced_lunch      0.000    0.584

The pattern in the mean column is worth noting. Every coefficient is centered at zero except the grand intercept, so the prior mean vector describes a null, intercept-only model: no covariate has any effect, and no group effect varies with anything at level two. The prior therefore carries the same interpretation the null model does in classical statistics, and the analysis can be read as asking how far the data move each coefficient away from that null. What differs is that here the null is a starting point that the data revise rather than a hypothesis that is accepted or rejected.

This is the same default the glmbayes family of packages uses for ordinary iid regression: a multivariate normal prior on the regression coefficients, centered at the null model and calibrated from the data rather than specified by hand. What the mixed model adds is that the specification is repeated once per group effect.

The setup is built by two calls. Prior_Setup_GLMM() performs the calibration, and pfamily_list() wraps the result as the Block 2 prior families the sampler consumes:

ps <- Prior_Setup_GLMM(form, data = dat, pop.pwt = 0.01)
pf <- pfamily_list(ps)

pop.pwt = 0.01 gives the prior on \(\gamma\) the weight of about one percent of the data. Because every component of pf comes back as dNormal, the variance components are known, which is what the first table above reports.

2 Estimating the model using the default settings

The sampler takes the design and the two prior objects directly:

fit <- rlmerb(
  n              = 1000,
  design         = model_setup(form, data = dat),
  pfamily_list   = pf,
  dispprior_list = list(dispersion = ps$group.dispersion),
  sim_method     = "DEFAULT"
)

The design argument carries the data and both design matrices, pfamily_list supplies the Block 2 priors from the previous section, and dispprior_list supplies the fixed \(\sigma^2\). Passing a bare list(dispersion = ...) rather than a dGamma() prior is what declares that dispersion known.

Under this specification the joint posterior over the group effects and the population effects is exactly multivariate normal, which admits a direct sampling scheme with no Markov chain. sim_method = "DEFAULT" selects it.

The same model is fitted again later by a two-block Gibbs sampler. Running both against one posterior is deliberate: the answer here is exact, so it serves as the standard against which the iterative sampler is judged.

3 Properties of the posterior and the iid sampling procedure

Collect the group effects as \(\beta = (\beta_1', \dots, \beta_J')'\). Up to an additive constant, the log posterior splits into the three pieces of the model:

\[ -2\log \pi(\gamma, \beta \mid y) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{j} \left\|y_j - D_j\beta_j\right\|^2}_{\text{(i) log-likelihood}} + \underbrace{\sum_{j} (\beta_j - \mathcal{W}_j\gamma)' \Psi^{-1} (\beta_j - \mathcal{W}_j\gamma)}_{\text{(ii) prior on the group effects}} + \underbrace{(\gamma - \mu_0)' V^{-1} (\gamma - \mu_0)}_{\text{(iii) prior on the population effects}}. \]

Because \(\sigma^2\), \(\Psi\) and \(V\) are all known, every term is quadratic in \((\gamma, \beta)\), and a quadratic log density is a normal density. The posterior is therefore multivariate normal, and it can be factorized into a marginal for \(\gamma\) and a conditional for \(\beta\).

3.1 Marginal distribution of the population effects

Integrating the group effects out of (i) and (ii) is a linear-Gaussian calculation. Substituting \(\beta_j = \mathcal{W}_j\gamma + u_j\) with \(u_j \sim N(0, \Psi)\) gives

\[ y_j \mid \gamma \sim N\!\left(D_j\mathcal{W}_j\,\gamma,\; V_j\right), \qquad V_j = \sigma^2 I_{n_j} + D_j \Psi D_j', \]

so each group contributes a Gaussian likelihood in \(\gamma\) with design \(X_j = D_j\mathcal{W}_j\). Combining these with (iii) gives the marginal posterior

\[ \gamma \mid y \sim N\!\left(m_\gamma,\; P_\gamma^{-1}\right), \qquad \begin{aligned} P_\gamma &= V^{-1} + \sum_j X_j' V_j^{-1} X_j, \\ m_\gamma &= P_\gamma^{-1}\Big(V^{-1}\mu_0 + \sum_j X_j' V_j^{-1} y_j\Big). \end{aligned} \]

The term \(D_j \Psi D_j'\) in \(V_j\) is what distinguishes this from ordinary regression: it inflates the variance of the groups that would otherwise be most influential, which is how the model discounts a group that is unusual rather than merely large.

3.2 Conditional distribution of the group effects

Given \(\gamma\), terms (i) and (ii) separate across groups, so the group effects are conditionally independent — each is an ordinary conjugate normal update, combining that group’s own data with the population prediction \(\mathcal{W}_j\gamma\):

\[ \beta_j \mid \gamma, y \sim N\!\left(b_j,\; \Sigma_j\right), \qquad \begin{aligned} \Sigma_j &= \left(\sigma^{-2} D_j' D_j + \Psi^{-1}\right)^{-1}, \\ b_j &= \Sigma_j\left(\sigma^{-2} D_j' y_j + \Psi^{-1}\mathcal{W}_j\gamma\right). \end{aligned} \]

The precision weights in \(b_j\) are the shrinkage: a group with plenty of data has a large \(D_j' D_j\) and stays near its own fit, while a small group is pulled toward \(\mathcal{W}_j\gamma\).

3.3 Drawing from the posterior

The two results above factorize the posterior exactly,

\[ \pi(\gamma, \beta \mid y) = \pi(\gamma \mid y)\;\prod_j \pi(\beta_j \mid \gamma, y), \]

so one draw from the joint posterior is obtained in two steps: draw \(\gamma\) from its marginal, then draw each \(\beta_j\) from its conditional given that value. Repeating the pair produces independent draws from the target itself — no burn-in to discard, no autocorrelation, and no convergence rate to bound. It also makes the ICM point estimate the exact posterior mean, since the mode and mean of a normal distribution coincide.

4 Summarizing the output

summary() collects the draws into tables:

summary(fit, groups = c("30", "15"))

The output is organized by block, following the structure of the model. After the call and a header giving the number of draws, the engine used and the data dimensions, it reports in turn:

  • Random effects — the reference lmer variance components, for orientation.
  • Block 2 variance components — the \(\tau^2_k\) and the residual \(\sigma^2\), with their prior family. Here everything is fixed, so the posterior tables are suppressed and a note says so.
  • Block 2 population effects — three tables for \(\gamma\): the prior means and standard deviations alongside the lmer reference, then the posterior mode, mean, standard deviation and Monte Carlo error, then distribution percentiles. Rows are named component::parameter, following the indexing described in Chapter-C01.
  • Block 1 group effects — the distribution of the per-group posterior modes across all 34 groups, followed by per-group detail for any levels named in groups.

The groups argument is optional; without it the per-group detail is replaced by a note pointing at fit$groupef.mode. The two levels requested here are the largest and smallest schools in the data.

Two features of the population effect tables follow from the default pop.pwt = 0.01. Because the prior is given the weight of only about one percent of the data, it barely competes with the likelihood: the posterior means land close to the lmer estimates, displaced slightly toward the prior means of the previous section. Since those prior means are the null model, that displacement is a mild shrinkage toward zero for every coefficient but the intercept.

Read on the scale that matters, the two fits agree throughout. Every Bayesian estimate falls within a fifth of a standard error of its lmer counterpart, and most within a twentieth, so no coefficient in the table supports a different reading under one fit than the other. Exact agreement was never expected: the prior carries a small amount of weight, and this chapter fixes the variance components where lmer estimates them, so the two are answering slightly different questions. The differences that remain are too small to be worth interpreting individually.

The Pr(Prior_tail) column has a related reading. For every row it measures the same thing: the posterior mass falling on the opposite side of the prior mean from the posterior estimate. It is thus a one-sided statement about how far the data have moved a coefficient away from its prior value, and how much posterior belief remains on the other side of it.

Because the prior means are the null model, that reference point is zero for every coefficient except the intercept, so for those rows the column answers the same question a one-sided p-value does. Across them it stays in the same range as half the two-sided p-value the corresponding lmer fit would report, typically running somewhat smaller because the posterior standard deviations are not identical to the lmer standard errors, and the ordering it induces matches the classical one. The intercept is read the same way, but against the calibrated prior mean rather than the origin, so it is not comparable to a test against zero.

5 Appendix: the reference lmer fit

The lmer columns used throughout this chapter come from a single REML fit that model_setup() performs on all observations. Prior_Setup_GLMM() returns it as mer_fit, and the same object is stored on the design, so either of these reaches it:

ps$mer_fit
design$lmer

It serves two purposes: it calibrates the default priors of the first section, and it populates the reference columns in the summary tables. Its own summary is:

Two points connect this back to the chapter. First, the fixed effects are reported in lmer’s flat parameterization, with the cross-level term written as free_reduced_lunch:distracted_a1. The summary tables carry the same seven coefficients but regroup them as component::parameter, so that interaction appears as distracted_a1::free_reduced_lunch — a level-two predictor of the distracted_a1 slope. Chapter-C01 covers the mapping.

Second, the Random effects block is the quantity this chapter holds fixed, and it is worth seeing that it is not fixed at these values:

round(C02$summary$tau2_prior_overview[, c("E[tau2]", "lmer")], 2)
#>                 E[tau2]   lmer
#> (Intercept)      159.14 191.79
#> distracted_ppvt    6.74  26.03
#> distracted_a1     11.48  41.05
#> Residual         388.80 366.41

The residual variance is calibrated close to the REML value, and the intercept variance is moderately tighter, but the two slope components are held near a quarter of what lmer estimates. This is the one place where the two fits are far apart, and it is a difference in specification rather than in estimation: a smaller \(\Psi\) shrinks the group effects harder toward their population predictions, so the group effects reported in the previous section are more tightly pooled than lmer would make them.

That the population effects nonetheless agree so closely is worth noting. Holding \(\Psi\) at these values costs little there, which is what makes this specification a reasonable starting point even though it is not the one lmer fits. The later chapters estimate the variance components rather than fixing them, and are the place to look for what changes when the Random effects block above is treated as unknown.
